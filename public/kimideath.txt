Western theory of death has largely excluded the psychedelic experience because it was built on a worldview that pathologizes non-ordinary states of consciousness (NOSC). From the 17th-century Newtonian-Cartesian model onward, Western science treated consciousness as a by-product of brain chemistry and dismissed any state that contradicted this—mystical, near-death, or psychedelic—as hallucination, delirium or psychosis. Psychiatry codified this exclusion by labeling all NOSC “mental disorder,” while drug laws criminalized the very tools that reliably produce death-rehearsal experiences. The result is a cultural feedback loop: because these states are taboo, they rarely enter academic discourse; because they are absent from discourse, they remain taboo.


Meanwhile, pre-industrial cultures used psychedelics and other NOSC technologies as “practice deaths.” Initiates symbolically died, reviewed their lives, met ancestors, and felt the dissolution of ego long before biological demise. These rituals generated collective maps of the dying process—Tibetan Bardo texts, Egyptian Books of the Dead, shamanic descent narratives—that Western thanatology only began to notice after 1960s hospice and near-death research forced the door open. Even now, most Western death models (Kübler-Ross stages, palliative sedation, advance directives) still frame death as a medical event to be managed, not a consciousness transition to be explored.


In short, the psychedelic layer is missing because Western theory was engineered to suppress it; the culture that could have integrated it spent four centuries outlawing the experiences, ridiculing the reports, and building hospitals instead of temples.
